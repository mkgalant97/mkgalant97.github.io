Atmospheric science

Research

Alongside music, I work in atmospheric and environmental science. My research focuses on measuring how much water the land around the Great Salt Lake loses to the air, a key unknown in managing a lake that has been shrinking for decades.

M.S. Thesis · Atmospheric Sciences

Evaluating Evaporation from Land Surfaces near the Great Salt Lake

University of Utah · August 2025 · Advisor: Dr. John D. Horel · Committee: Dr. Sebastian Hoch, Dr. Kevin Perry

A pilot study for the Utah Division of Water Resources that tested two eddy covariance systems, the established Campbell Scientific EC150 and LI-COR's lower-cost LI-710, at an exposed playa in Farmington Bay and between mineral evaporation ponds at Compass Minerals. The aim: find out whether the cheaper sensor can be trusted to monitor evaporation across the Great Salt Lake basin.

Why it matters

Terminal saline lakes are drying worldwide, and the Great Salt Lake (the largest in North America) has been losing volume since the 1980s, largely due to consumptive water use. The newly exposed lakebed threatens wildlife, agriculture, and the air quality of the nearly three million people living along the Wasatch Front.

Evaporation is one of the biggest and least-measured terms in the lake's water budget. Within the lake's historic boundary, exposed playa now covers about 23% of the area, wetlands 26%, and diked mineral ponds about 6%. Each loses water at a different rate, so accurate, surface-specific measurements are essential for water policy.

What I did

We installed two 3-meter flux towers in 2024: UUCMF, on a berm between two mineral ponds in Weber County, and UUPYF, on the Farmington Bay playa (reached by a 3 km bike ride or walk). Each tower carries both an EC150 (3-D wind, open-path gas analyzer) and an LI-710 (1-D vertical wind with a fast-response humidity sensor), powered by solar panels and streaming data in real time through the UUNET network.

My analysis combined each vendor's diagnostics with turbulence-based grades (stationarity, integral turbulence, and wind direction tests), then compared sensible heat (H), latent heat (LE), and evaporation between the two systems by site, time of day, and boundary-layer stability.

Key findings

  1. 01

    The LI-710 underestimates evaporation

    Sensible heat from the two systems agreed closely at both sites, but the LI-710 consistently reported lower latent heat flux and evaporation than the EC150, with the gap growing over the season. That points to the water-vapor pathway (the humidity sensor or air intake) rather than the wind measurement. LI-COR has since issued a firmware update to be evaluated next.

  2. 02

    Surface type shapes the turbulence

    The playa swings between dry, muddy, flooded, and frozen, producing more frequent very stable (9% vs. 4%) and very unstable (16% vs. 10%) conditions than the mineral ponds, whose water moderates temperature and keeps the boundary layer closer to neutral.

  3. 03

    Strict quality control discards a lot

    More than half of the EC150's 30-minute latent heat estimates failed the standard turbulence tests, especially at night and at the playa. Dropping them biases daily totals low and averages high, so future work should treat the filtering itself as a source of uncertainty.

  4. 04

    First cool-season estimates

    After quality control, the EC150 measured about 0.52 mm/day of evaporation from the playa and 0.53 mm/day near the mineral ponds from October to March. These are likely lower bounds that will be compared against warm-season data and earlier studies.

What's next

Measurements continued through October 2025 to cover a full annual cycle. Next steps include testing LI-COR's firmware fix during the warm season, quantifying how much evaporation is hidden in low-turbulence periods, comparing tower estimates with satellite and model products, and adding salinity measurements at the mineral ponds.
